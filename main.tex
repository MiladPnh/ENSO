\section*{Key Points}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A 32-dimensional learned latent state advanced by a seasonally modulated linear
        operator forecasts Ni\~no 3.4 to ACC 0.62 over leads 1--18
  \item Skill gains over an observable-predictor benchmark are specific to El Ni\~no events,
        boreal-winter targets, and leads 12--17
  \item The forecast's amplitude shortfall is a blend-weight calibration choice, not a
        dynamical limit; restoring full amplitude costs 0.19 ACC
\end{itemize}

\begin{abstract}
Forecasting the El Ni\~no--Southern Oscillation beyond seasonal lead times generally requires
either dynamical models, which are physically complete but difficult to diagnose when they
fail, or learned models, which are often skilful but opaque. We take an intermediate approach:
a transformer autoencoder with circulation-shaped attention windows compresses thirteen
tropical-Pacific fields into a 32-dimensional latent state, which is then advanced by a
deliberately simple, seasonally modulated linear operator built from damped oscillators in a
learned orthogonal basis. Evaluated by four-fold temporal cross-validation with a 24-month
buffer and paired year-block bootstrap uncertainties, the system reaches an anomaly
correlation of 0.619 over leads 1--18, exceeding a four-predictor observational benchmark by
$0.054$ (95\% CI $[0.003, 0.104]$). Because the operator is structurally simple, its learned
dynamics can be read directly: all 40 trained models converge on a dominant quasi-biennial
mode at $2.41\pm0.04$ years which becomes the least damped of three, from an initialisation in
which all damping rates are identical. We report where this skill does and does not exist: the
advantage over the benchmark is confined to El Ni\~no events, boreal-winter targets, and leads
12--17, and three controlled ablations -- on latent dimension, oscillator count, and an
explicit amplitude nonlinearity -- return null results, the latter two under a paired,
multi-fold protocol that also demonstrates measured selection bias in single-fold model
development. Finally, we show that the system's well-known underestimation of event amplitude
is not a dynamical limitation but a calibration choice: the unblended operator already carries
0.73 of observed amplitude at lead 6, and recovering full amplitude costs 0.19 in anomaly
correlation.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Plain Language Summary}
El Ni\~no and La Ni\~na are the largest year-to-year swings in the Earth's climate, and
predicting them more than a few months ahead is difficult. Most forecasting systems are either
large physical simulations, which are expensive and hard to diagnose when they fail, or machine
learning models, which are accurate but difficult to interpret. We take an intermediate
approach: a neural network first compresses thirteen physical fields over the tropical Pacific
into just 32 numbers, and a second, deliberately simple model advances those 32 numbers forward
in time. Because that second model is simple, we can read off what it learned -- in particular,
an oscillation with a period of about 2.4 years that decays more slowly than the others. We
test the forecasts carefully, holding out four separate decades in turn, and we report not only
where the system works but where it does not: its advantage over a simple statistical baseline
is concentrated in warm events and in winter, and it systematically under-predicts how large
events become. We show that this last shortcoming is not a limitation of the model's physics
but a deliberate trade-off, and we measure exactly what correcting it would cost in accuracy.
